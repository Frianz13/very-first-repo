# Cold Outreach Pipeline for B2B SaaS — A Playbook

**Author :** Zahra Fitriansya

**Date :** August 5, 2026

**Built from :** the 11 practitioners collected in [`/research`](../research/sources.md)

## Scope, and who this is for

This playbook is written for a **first-time sender with no budget** : one person, one inbox, no paid data tools, no sales team, selling something to other businesses. That covers a solo founder doing their own outreach, and it covers an independent service provider, which is what I run myself. It does not describe a rep inside a funded sales team, and where the difference matters I say so. That constraint is deliberate. Most of the advice I collected assumes a funded B2B SaaS sales organisation, and a lot of it quietly depends on paid software.

For the same reason, **I define the trade vocabulary the first time it appears.** Two months ago I did not know what B2B, SaaS or a pipeline were, and every source in this collection writes as though the reader already does. A playbook addressed to a beginner that speaks only to insiders is not addressed to a beginner. Where a recommendation only works if you buy something, I say so, and I give the version that survives without it.

Every recommendation below cites the practitioner it came from. Where I reason about those sources rather than report them, the passage is marked 'My note'. Passages that only explain how to read this document carry no such label, because they make no claim about anyone's work. Where they contradict each other I don't average them : I pick a side and explain why, in [Where experts disagree](#b-where-experts-disagree). In two of the five, the disagreement turns out to be one of vocabulary or of scope rather than of substance, and I say that instead of inventing a winner.

## A. The pipeline

**How to read the stages.** Stages 1 and 2 are **set-up, done once** before any outreach happens : deciding who you are going after, and making sure your email can physically arrive. Stages 3 to 6 are the **loop you repeat for every prospect** : write, sequence, call, and, where the first three do not land, record.

**Where this playbook stops.** It ends at a booked conversation. Everything after the meeting is agreed, discovery, demo, negotiation, close, is a different discipline and is deliberately out of scope.

### Stage 1 — Choose who you contact, before writing anything

#### **1.1) Treat account selection as the highest-leverage decision in the whole pipeline.**
Not the copy, not the channel, not the tooling.
*(source: Mark Colgan, [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/outboundsalestech_outboundsales-b2bsales-salesstrategy-activity-7467175858944974848-TQ6P), post from 01.06.2026 : "Account selection is the single biggest performance lever. Not messaging, not channels, not tools.")*

#### **1.2) Enter an organisation from the bottom, not the top.**
Start with individual contributors to learn how the work actually runs. Use middle management to connect what you learned to a business problem. Approach executives last, by which point you can talk about their business rather than your product.
*(source: Kevin Dorsey, [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kddorsey3_let-me-give-you-one-of-the-most-powerful-activity-7123638669931868162-JuwF), evergreen post, c. 2023. LinkedIn does not expose an exact date)*

#### **1.3) Get the list right before you send to it, not after.**
Sending to inaccurate data damages your domain reputation and your brand at the same time, and it does so at the speed you scale. The cost is measurable : in a 5,224-email campaign, **40% of the negative replies were data-quality problems**, where the named contacts had left the company since joining the list. That campaign was otherwise well built, and the data is what broke it.
*(sources: Kevin Dorsey, [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kddorsey3_with-connect-rates-continuing-to-plummet-activity-6978002798965641217-4a89), evergreen post, c. 2023. LinkedIn does not expose an exact date ; Armand Farrokh & Nick Cegelski, ["How Many Meetings Can I Book Off 6,474 Personalized Cold Emails?"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xz1Aw4y3CdI), published 24.02.2026)*
> **My note :** Two independent sources reach this from opposite directions, one as a standing principle and one as a post-mortem on a real campaign, which is the strongest form of agreement in this collection. Neither says how to verify a list without paying for a tool. The free substitute is slower but real : open each contact's LinkedIn profile and confirm the role is still listed as current, with no end date, then cross-check against the company's own site or its LinkedIn people directory. **Do not use posting activity as the test.** Plenty of people never post for years while staying in the same job, and treating silence as departure would delete valid contacts.

#### **1.4) Per account, not per list : time-box the research to five minutes, and have a real reason before you make contact.**
The minimum is their website open in front of you. Beyond that, work a fixed order : news and press releases from the last six months, then the About page for stated goals, then the individual's LinkedIn. **If nothing usable appears in those three places within five minutes, move on to the next account.**
*(source: John Barrows, ["How to Research Prospects"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dwb0trBr6dU), published 21.12.2017)*


### Stage 2 — Make sure the message can physically arrive

#### **2.1) Treat deliverability as the bottleneck, not a technicality.**
*Deliverability means whether your email reaches the inbox at all, rather than the spam folder or nowhere.*
Warm a sending domain for 3+ months, rotate between secondary domains, and never bulk-send from a single inbox. *Warming means sending small and slowly growing volumes from a new address, so the mail providers learn it belongs to a real correspondent rather than a bulk sender.* A message that lands in spam has no copy problem to solve.
*(source: Will Allred, [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/williamallred_weve-run-some-cold-email-experiments-for-activity-7458187629520592896-PpGl), post from 12.05.2026)*
> **My note :** Every additional domain is a purchase, so this is partly outside the budget assumed here. A reader without one keeps the warming period and the low volume, and drops the rotation.
>
> None of the sources say how warming is actually done, which makes a three-month instruction impossible to follow. The mechanism is that a new domain has no history, so a provider seeing it send two hundred messages on its first day has nothing to weigh that against. What you are building is evidence of ordinary two-way correspondence. Done by hand, and free : send five to ten messages a day to people who genuinely know you, ask them to reply, because an address that only ever sends is the pattern being screened for, and raise the daily number slowly across weeks rather than in jumps. Paid warming software automates exactly this, at 25 to 50 messages a day ramping up, which is what the campaign in 2.3 used. The manual version costs time instead of money.

#### **2.2) Fix the fundamentals before automating any of it.**
Automating a broken process does not repair it. It reproduces the fault at scale.
*(source: Mark Colgan, [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/outboundsalestech_aiinsales-salesstrategy-b2bsales-activity-7465061731464224768-8GUs), post from 26.05.2026)*

#### **2.3) Send from more than one domain, and meter the daily volume.**
A published example of the mechanics : 20 separate sending domains, with sends metered at 25 to 50 emails per day per domain.
**The correct warming period is disputed.** That campaign warmed its domains for two weeks, against the 3+ months recommended in 2.1. See [Section B](#b-where-experts-disagree).
*(source: Armand Farrokh & Nick Cegelski, ["How Many Meetings Can I Book Off 6,474 Personalized Cold Emails?"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xz1Aw4y3CdI), published 24.02.2026)*
> **My note :** Twenty domains plus warming software is a paid setup. Stripped back to what costs nothing, the surviving principle is the metered daily volume in the sentence above, run on a single domain.

#### **2.4) Pilot on about 100 contacts before sending the full list.**
Their own stated regret after the campaign was that they should have sent to roughly 100 contacts first, which would have surfaced both the stale-data problem and the bad timing early, while the list was still intact.
*(source: Armand Farrokh & Nick Cegelski, ["How Many Meetings Can I Book Off 6,474 Personalized Cold Emails?"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xz1Aw4y3CdI), published 24.02.2026)*
> **My note :** The campaign itself ran on paid tooling, but the pilot principle does not depend on any of it : send your first hundred by hand before scaling anything.

#### **2.5) Check the calendar before you launch.**
Roughly 20% of the negative replies in that campaign were caused by nothing but timing. Sends landed either side of the Christmas break and returned out-of-office notices instead of answers.
*(source: Armand Farrokh & Nick Cegelski, ["How Many Meetings Can I Book Off 6,474 Personalized Cold Emails?"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xz1Aw4y3CdI), published 24.02.2026)*


### Stage 3 — Write the message

> This stage is built on the largest dataset in the collection : a study of **85 million cold emails**, run with **Gong**. *(My note : Bay names Gong as the research partner but does not explain the company. Gong sells revenue-intelligence software to sales teams, which is the likeliest reason a dataset of that size was available; I have not verified the figure independently and it should be read as the presenter's claim.)* The same study found the average sender needs **344 emails to earn one reply**, which is the number worth remembering before judging any single campaign.
>
> **My note :** Two figures in this stage describe different populations, and Bay does not flag the difference himself. The 344 figure is the average across all senders, good and bad. The 2% and 10% reply rates quoted in 3.3 come from a narrower comparison, between emails that carry buyer context and emails that do not. They are not the same baseline, and reading them as one series would overstate what any single change achieves.
>
> **A note on the source, to avoid confusion.** The masterclass is presented by **Jason Bay** of Outbound Squad, but it is published on the **30 Minutes to President's Club** channel, whose own hosts, Armand Farrokh and Nick Cegelski, are cited separately in 1.3 and 2.3 to 2.5. Two different practitioners, one channel.
>
> Bay's framework gives the email five parts. Items 3.2 to 3.6 take those five parts in order and bring in the other practitioners where they add something to a specific part. Item 3.7 sits outside the framework and applies to the whole message.

#### **3.1) Build every cold email from the same five parts, in this order.**
Subject line, relevance, problem, value and social proof, call to action. *The call to action, usually shortened to CTA, is the single thing you ask the reader to do.*
*(source: Jason Bay, ["Cold Email Masterclass"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JX1UNIJwcCY), published 05.05.2026)*

#### **3.2) Part one, the subject line : one to four words, lowercase, in the buyer's own vocabulary.**
Its only job is to earn the open, so it should not mention your product. Match the way colleagues write to each other internally. Avoid marketing language, numbers, percentages and questions. Do not put social proof here; it works in the body but not in the subject. **All-lowercase subject lines show the highest open rates**, sentence case next. Capitalising every word performs worst, because no colleague writes that way.
*(source: Jason Bay, ["Cold Email Masterclass"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JX1UNIJwcCY), published 05.05.2026)*

#### **3.3) Part two, relevance : open with the reason you are writing to this specific buyer.**
Including a trigger or a piece of context about the buyer raises reply rates roughly **fivefold**, from about 2% to about 10%. **For director-and-above personas, reference something happening at the company rather than something about the individual : company-level context produces a 50% higher reply rate.**
*(source: Jason Bay, ["Cold Email Masterclass"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JX1UNIJwcCY), published 05.05.2026)*

> **Two other practitioners land in the same place from a different direction.** John Barrows argues that if you are forced to choose between personalisation and relevance you should take relevance, because a relevant question can be asked of fifty comparable buyers while a personal detail cannot be reused, and research time does not scale : *"Just because you know I went to the University of Maryland does not buy you any points these days."* Will Allred adds that the same product means different things to different seats, so three people in the same finance department should not receive identical messaging : a **CFO**, the executive who owns the company's finances, is thinking about a multi-year plan ; an **FP&A director**, who runs financial planning and analysis, the team that builds the forecasts, is thinking about how unreliable those forecasts are ; and a **finance operations analyst**, the person doing that work by hand every day, is thinking about the manual work itself.
> Read together with Bay's figures, these are not three positions but one : personal trivia is dead, business context is not. The argument is treated properly in [Section B](#b-where-experts-disagree).
> *(sources: John Barrows, ["Personalization vs Relevance"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Cqw1jBYOm4U), published 29.03.2026 ; Will Allred, [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/williamallred_one-of-the-biggest-problems-with-your-cold-activity-7442903238540013568-A5Ux), post from 15.04.2026)*

#### **3.4) Part three, the problem : name it plainly, and never in buzzwords.**
Leading with problems and priorities lifts reply rates by **more than 20%**. Words like *AI*, *platform*, *ROI* and *all-in-one* measurably reduce them.
*(source: Jason Bay, ["Cold Email Masterclass"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JX1UNIJwcCY), published 05.05.2026)*

> **How to phrase it.** Jed Mahrle builds this part as a diagnostic question, and the whole message lives or dies on getting that question right. It has to be specific enough to be answerable : *"Are you struggling to keep up with the competition?"* is ignorable, while *"Can you tell from your CRM which accounts are close to churning before they tell you?"* makes the reader stop, because it names a gap they can go and check.
> Will Allred adds a useful test : good copy answers the objections the reader never says out loud, such as *"I'm not the right person"* or *"we already have a vendor"*, instead of manufacturing urgency.
> *(sources: Jed Mahrle, [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/outboundsales_sales-activity-7462125341667409920-yE-0), post from 25.05.2026, and [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/outboundsales_sales-activity-7465024444596486145-15qR), post from 01.06.2026 ; Will Allred, [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/williamallred_when-was-the-last-time-you-thought-about-activity-7459588294826168320-rGmL), post from 15.05.2026)*

#### **3.5) Part four, value and social proof : say what you actually do for them, then name a company that resembles them.**
*Social proof means naming an existing customer as evidence that someone comparable already trusted you.* Both halves are needed. State the value specifically rather than in general terms, and pair it with a customer the reader can measure themselves against. Referencing a comparable customer lifts replies by as much as **41%**, but the comparison has to be credible : naming Amazon to a small training firm does not work, while naming another small training firm does. The purpose of this part is to show the reader you are a peer.
*(source: Jason Bay, ["Cold Email Masterclass"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JX1UNIJwcCY), published 05.05.2026)*

#### **3.6) Part five, the call to action : use exactly one, and do not ask for time.**
Asking for time is the worst-performing CTA in the data, reducing reply rate by as much as **44%**, so the thing you actually want is the thing you should not ask for. Open-ended questions are almost as bad in email, because answering costs effort. Interest-based asks answerable with yes or no perform well. **Offer-based CTAs perform best of all, lifting replies by up to 28%.**
Three kinds of offer, in ascending difficulty. **Sell the introduction :** offer the expertise of the person they would be meeting. **One-to-many :** a reusable insight that still feels specific, such as research across 300 companies in their sector. **One-to-one :** something built for them alone, such as a recorded walkthrough of a flaw in their own checkout flow.
*(source: Jason Bay, ["Cold Email Masterclass"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JX1UNIJwcCY), published 05.05.2026)*

#### **3.7) Whatever the parts say, keep the whole thing readable on a phone without scrolling.**
Four to five sentences, under 100 words, one or two short sentences per paragraph, plenty of white space.
*(source: Jason Bay, ["Cold Email Masterclass"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JX1UNIJwcCY), published 05.05.2026)*


### Stage 4 — Sequence the cold touches, then follow up on the warm ones

#### **4.1) Plan five to eight touches, and give each one a different piece of value.**
Split the value proposition across the sequence instead of loading it into the first message.
*(source: John Barrows, ["Strategic Contact Strategy for Tier 1 Accounts"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EDkjrZWyPUU), published 15.12.2017)*

#### **4.2) In a cold sequence, never reference a failed attempt.**
No *"did you get my first email"*, no *"bubbling this one up to the top"*. It adds nothing for the reader and marks the message as a follow-up before they have read it. **This holds for cold contact only. After a real conversation the opposite applies, see 4.5.**
*(source: John Barrows, ["Strategic Contact Strategy for Tier 1 Accounts"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EDkjrZWyPUU), published 15.12.2017. Note the age of this source; see [Section F](#f-who-i-would-not-recommend-following-and-why))*

#### **4.3) Connect email and calling into one pipeline rather than running them separately.**
Call the people who already replied, while the reply is still fresh, and use what they wrote as the context for the call.
*(source: Jed Mahrle, [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/outboundsales_cold-call-script-generator-activity-7452340618049204224-Zc02), post from 15.05.2026)*
> **My note :** Mahrle's version routes replies into Slack automatically, which is a paid setup. Without it, check replies at a fixed time each day and call back the same day; the speed matters, the automation does not.

#### **4.4) Send a summary email after any real conversation.**
Say during the call that you are going to send it. Then write it immediately, and keep it to **their** situation, timeline and priorities rather than your value proposition. Ask them to reply and correct anything you got wrong.
*(source: John Barrows, ["How to Follow Up When You Get Ghosted"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ImjXwvqDtM), published 14.04.2026)*

#### **4.5) If they then disappear, reply to that same email rather than starting a new one.**
Assume something ordinary happened : *"Not sure what happened. Let me know when you want to reschedule."* Keep replying to the same thread so the whole sequence stays visible in one place. Late in the sequence, change the subject line to **"Still interested?"** and ask them to answer either way, *"so I don't continue with unnecessary follow-up."* Barrows keeps one further message after that, with the subject line changed again to *"Did I lose you?"*
*(source: John Barrows, ["How to Follow Up When You Get Ghosted"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ImjXwvqDtM), published 14.04.2026)*

### Stage 5 — The call, and the objection that follows it

> **My note : on a dependency none of the sources state.** This stage assumes you have a working phone number for the buyer. Direct-dial numbers are normally the output of a paid data tool, which puts the whole stage partly outside the scope set at the top. A reader without one is limited to numbers published on company websites, and should expect to reach a switchboard rather than the person.

#### **5.1) Open with a purposeful line, not a courtesy one.**
*"Thanks for taking my call"* instead of *"how are you doing today"*. *"Do you have a few moments"* instead of *"is this a good time"*. *"The reason for my call is"* instead of *"just checking in"*. Weak openers announce that there is no reason for the call.
*(source: John Barrows, ["Starting with Powerful Introductions"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eiHUey6h_cY), published 16.02.2018. Note the age of this source; see [Section F](#f-who-i-would-not-recommend-following-and-why))*

> **My note : because 5.1 and 5.2 come from opposite instincts.** Barrows hands you exact wording and tells you to control the opening. Braun's entire approach is built against scripting, so that the person on the other end does not feel handled. Read as consecutive steps they give a reader whiplash : memorise these lines, then do not sound rehearsed.
> They are compatible, but only if you know which one governs when. **Barrows owns the first ten seconds**, where you have to state a reason before anyone will keep listening. **Braun owns everything after that**, where the job stops being delivery and becomes asking. The mistake is carrying the scripted posture past the opening, which is exactly what Braun is warning about.
> The deeper split is treated in [Section B](#b-where-experts-disagree).

#### **5.2) Draw information out instead of pushing it in.**
Raise one possible problem without assuming they have it, ask how they handle it, then stop talking. *"Buyers have the answers. Sellers have the questions."*
*(source: Josh Braun, ["How to Make a Cold Call"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hpXev0vUcvQ), published 26.03.2026)*

#### **5.3) Detach from the result of any single call.**
Braun's framing is that even the best baseball players reach base only about a third of the time, so striking out and getting on base are parts of the same activity. Over 46 calls he finds there are reliably two or three real conversations; over three calls there may be none, and that means nothing.
*(source: Josh Braun, ["How to Be Relaxed & Chill Cold Calling"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jmkjpDaic2o), published 21.03.2026)*
> **My note :** He offers the batting figure from memory and hedges it himself, and the 46 is his own experience rather than a study. Treat the shape of the argument as the useful part, not the numbers, the same standard applied to 5.4 below.

#### **5.4) When the objection comes : acknowledge, label, redirect, then ask.**
Acknowledge what they said, name the objection out loud, use *"that's actually why I called"*, and finish with an open question. Reported to move conversation-to-meeting rate from 7% to 15%.
*(source: Florin Tatulea, [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/florintatulea_heres-a-stupid-simple-4-step-framework-to-activity-7407121355948593152-eX8c), post from 15.01.2026. The figure is published without a stated sample or method; see [Section F](#f-who-i-would-not-recommend-following-and-why))*


### Stage 6 — An alternative channel : video

> **My note :** Every other stage eventually asks for sending infrastructure : a warmed domain, months of patience, tooling. Video asks for a phone and a face. It still needs someone to send it to, so Stage 1 still applies, but nothing between the recording and the recipient has to be bought. For the reader this playbook is written for, that makes it the cheapest way in.

#### **6.1) Treat a prospecting video as a cold call that happens to have a face.**
Not a production. The same words that would work on a call work on camera, described as *"a virtual handshake, or just a live cold call where it's just your face."* Reframing it this way removes the blocker most people actually report, which is not skill but perfectionism.
*(source: Morgan J. Ingram, ["The Video Prospecting Playbook That Actually Works"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=81vTeS2Phu4), published 09.04.2026)*

#### **6.2) Record on a phone, not in software.**
A phone camera reads as more natural and more human than a screen-recording tool, and it converts better when sent through LinkedIn. No subscription is required to start.
*(source: Morgan J. Ingram, ["The Video Prospecting Playbook That Actually Works"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=81vTeS2Phu4), published 09.04.2026)*

#### **6.3) Look at the lens, not at yourself.**
Watching your own face while recording is what produces endless retakes. Looking at the lens makes it feel like addressing a person, and it is the practical cure for the perfectionism above.
*(source: Morgan J. Ingram, ["The Video Prospecting Playbook That Actually Works"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=81vTeS2Phu4), published 09.04.2026)*

#### **6.4) Stand up, smile, and face a natural light source.**
Standing opens the diaphragm and audibly improves the delivery. On expression : *"most people when they do a video, they look like they're being interrogated."* Good audio and daylight matter more than any equipment.
*(source: Morgan J. Ingram, ["The Video Prospecting Playbook That Actually Works"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=81vTeS2Phu4), published 09.04.2026)*

#### **6.5) Structure the video with KPIC.**
**K**now, show you researched the organisation and the person. **P**roblem, name a real pain. **I**mpact, what the problem costs and what fixing it is worth. **C**all to action, a natural and unforced ask.
*(source: Kevin Dorsey, [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kddorsey3_i-got-the-best-cold-video-outreach-in-years-activity-7039574915628351488-GMpJ), evergreen post, c. 2023. LinkedIn does not expose an exact date)*
